About this analysis

This page communicates a provisional interpretive synthesis. The underlying analysis draws on participant material; the page presents the analyst’s interpretation of it (discussion paper, 8 October 2026), not the material itself.

All text is analyst paraphrase. No verbatim quotations, participant names, case facts, counts or proportions appear, and nothing here shows how common or how strongly evidenced any idea is. A quotation would be added only after its text, source locator, context and publication permission were checked against primary material; the page’s evidence record is intentionally empty until then.

The diagrams are illustrative inquiry scaffolds and metaphors. They are not participant cases or proven chains of causation; connections run in several directions, and mediation, feedback and non-linearity are expected. The perspective lenses in chapter 3 are illustrative analytical lenses built from the paper’s propositions; they are not participant accounts, and people in similar roles may see things very differently.

Paper sections drawn on: standpoint (2); propositions (3.1–3.6); mediation and context (4–7); revision criteria (8); webpage experiment (10); participatory analysis (12). Chapter-level section references beyond those in the propositions should be confirmed against the paper. This page stores and submits nothing; prompts can be used aloud or in an existing workshop platform.

    4 · Who can shape decisions

    Being present does not settle who decides.

    Someone becomes involved. Then ask which of these remain open to them. The six lines are listed alphabetically; they are not stages, scores or a ladder, and they can diverge within the same project.

    Participation and authority

    Taking part is not the same as being able to change the question, the budget or the interpretation. People may still exercise agency through routes the formal arrangements do not show.

    Resources and power

    Resources can move while control over questions, budgets and interpretation stays elsewhere. Reciprocal arrangements exist; unequal power is not inevitable.

    Formal structures and functioning relationships

    Formal structures show who is named as responsible. Day-to-day work may rely on relationships and informal labour that they do not show. That labour can signal unequal burden, not a model to copy.

    5 · What changes across contexts

    What travels — and what changes?

    A practice may travel while its meaning, feasibility, cost and consequences change. Context helps constitute the phenomenon; it is not just a backdrop. Select a point on the path.

    Metaphor: overlapping loops are not separate bounded systems or levels, and distant decisions can shape immediate experience. No countries are compared and no best practice is implied. The dashed circle suggests the practice may look different on arrival; some of it may also carry across, or be reshaped through local agency. Terminology, including UK terms, may not travel unchanged. Sections 5–7.

    6 · What needs revising

    What can you see that this account cannot?

    Strengthen

    What does the account explain particularly well, and from which position do you see that?

    Qualify

    Where does it depend on particular positions, conditions or terminology?

    Challenge

    What is absent, distorted or inadequately explained? What counterexample would matter?

    Still open

    • Which propositions hold, change or fall away when checked against primary material?
    • Which contexts, knowledge traditions or practices does this vocabulary fail to see?
    • Where do propositions overlap enough that they should be merged, separated or reworded?

    Responses can inform further interpretation. Agreement is not statistical validation, frequency is not significance, disagreement should stay visible, and no one contributor speaks for a country or group.
